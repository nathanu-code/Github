# The Fuel You Already Stockpiled Is the Wrong One

*Article. Data from [`products/stoves-and-fuel.md`](../products/stoves-and-fuel.md).*

---

Ask someone what fuel they're storing for an emergency and the answer is usually
gasoline. Cans in the garage, rotated when someone remembers.

Gasoline has a shelf life of **three to six months.**

Modern pump gas is ethanol-blended, and ethanol is hygroscopic — it pulls water
out of the air, straight through the vent of the can it's sitting in. Twelve
months is the absolute ceiling under good conditions. Six is realistic. Three is
common.

So the most widely held emergency fuel is the fastest-spoiling option available.
Meanwhile the fuel that wins on every single axis is sitting in a steel bottle
behind every hardware store in the country.

## Propane wins on everything at once

This basically never happens in product comparisons. There's normally a
trade-off — cheaper but heavier, lighter but pricier. Propane just wins:

| | Propane |
|---|---|
| **Shelf life** | Indefinite. The tank is what expires, and galvanized tanks go 30+ years |
| **Cost per BTU** | Cheapest available. ~$1.30/lb refilled |
| **Cold performance** | Boiling point −44 °F. Works anywhere you can survive |
| **Availability** | Refillable at hardware stores, gas stations, U-Haul |
| **Appliances** | Stoves, heaters, lanterns, generators, water heaters |

There is no catch, only a constraint: it needs a heavy steel cylinder. For a
backpack that's disqualifying. For a garage it's irrelevant.

## What everything else costs you

**Shelf life:**

| Fuel | Lasts |
|---|---|
| Propane | Indefinite |
| Alcohol | Indefinite |
| Butane canisters | ~8 years (manufacturer figure), cool and dry |
| Kerosene | ~5 years, longer with annual stabiliser |
| White gas | 5–7 years sealed — but **~2 years once opened** |
| Gasoline | **3–6 months** |

Note the white gas cliff. Coleman fuel is a solid long-term store *sealed*;
crack the can and you've started a two-year clock. If your plan involves a
partly-used can from a camping trip three summers ago, you don't have fuel — you
have a can.

## The cold-weather trap

This is the one that catches people, because it's invisible when you buy and
absolute when you need it.

| Fuel | Stops vaporizing below |
|---|---|
| Propane | −44 °F |
| Isobutane | ~11 °F |
| **n-Butane** | **~30 °F** |

A butane canister stove is a **paperweight below freezing.** Not weak — it
doesn't light. The liquid inside won't become gas, so there's nothing to burn.
Butane stoves genuinely want temperatures above 50 °F; isobutane blends hold to
roughly 15 °F.

There's a nastier second-order effect. Canister fuel is a *blend* of propane,
isobutane and n-butane. In the cold the propane fraction — the part that still
vaporizes — burns off first and disproportionately. Canister pressure drops as
you use it, so **a half-empty canister performs worse than a fresh one at the
same temperature.** Your stove gets weaker exactly as your situation gets
longer.

Pure propane isn't sold in lightweight canisters because it needs far higher
storage pressure, and that means heavier steel. Every canister stove is a
compromise made for weight. If you aren't carrying it, don't accept the
compromise.

## The cost gap is bigger than it looks

| Option | Rate |
|---|---|
| 20 lb propane tank | ~$1.30/lb refilled |
| 1 lb camping canister | **6–10× more per gallon than bulk propane** |
| 1 lb butane canister | ≥2× a 16.4 oz propane cylinder, per BTU |

Six to ten times. Convenience fuel is priced like convenience food, and a
stockpile is exactly the case where you're buying enough for the multiple to
matter. If you like the canister form factor, refillable 1 lb cylinders exist
and pay back fast.

## Buy layers, not a winner

Propane is the answer to "what do I stockpile," not to every question. Pick a
set whose failure conditions don't overlap:

1. **Propane, 20 lb tanks** — the workhorse. 90% of your cooking. Store
   outdoors or in a detached ventilated space, never a basement.
2. **A wood stove — rocket or gasifier.** The fuel is free, unlimited, and
   cannot expire. It's the only layer with no supply chain at all.
3. **Denatured alcohol.** Indefinite shelf life, no moving parts, nothing to
   break. Low output, but it's the backup that can't fail mechanically.
4. **A multi-fuel liquid stove** if you want flexibility — the Primus OmniFuel
   switches between gas and liquid better than anything else; the MSR
   WhisperLite Universal is lighter and quieter.

Propane runs out. Wood doesn't. Alcohol can't break. Nothing common takes out
all three.

## And separate storing from carrying

These are different purchases for different jobs, and conflating them is how
people end up with the wrong fuel:

- **Storing** → propane. Weight is free in a garage.
- **Carrying** → isobutane canisters or a multi-fuel liquid stove. Weight is
  everything in a bag.

Buying a butane car-camping stove and calling it your emergency fuel plan gets
you the worst cold performance, the highest cost per BTU, and a dependency on a
canister standard that may not be available where you are.

## The short version

Stop rotating gasoline. Buy three 20 lb propane tanks and a burner — under $250
all in — and add a wood stove for the day the tanks run dry.

Then buy a $40 TDS meter for your water gear, because that's the other thing
nobody verifies until it matters.
