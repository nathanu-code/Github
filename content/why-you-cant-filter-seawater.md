# Why You Can't Filter Salt Out of Seawater

*Long-form article. Sources in [`products/desalination-buyers-guide.md`](../products/desalination-buyers-guide.md).*

---

You are surrounded by water you cannot drink. It is the oldest cruel joke in
survival, and the instinct it produces is always the same: *there must be a
filter for this.*

There isn't. Not because nobody has built a fine enough one, but because
filtering is the wrong verb.

## Salt doesn't float in water. It disappears into it.

When salt dissolves, it does not become tiny suspended specks drifting in the
water. The crystal comes apart. Sodium and chloride separate into individual
charged ions, each one wrapping itself in a shell of water molecules that are
electrically attracted to its charge.

What you are left with is not water with salt *in* it. It is a single uniform
liquid in which sodium, chloride, and water are interleaved at molecular scale.

This is why the filter instinct fails. A filter is a sieve: it works when the
thing you want to remove is physically larger than the thing you want to keep.
A hydrated sodium ion is roughly the size of the water molecules around it. A
mesh fine enough to stop the salt stops the water too. There is no gap to
exploit — there is nothing to strain.

Every survival straw, gravity bag, ceramic element, and purification tablet in
your kit works on things that are *bigger* than water: bacteria, protozoa,
sediment, some viruses. Against dissolved salt, all of them do exactly nothing.
Drinking through one is drinking seawater.

## There are only two ways out

Two mechanisms actually separate salt from water. Every real desalination
device on earth — from a $400 hand pump to a municipal plant serving a city —
is one of these two.

### 1. Force the water through anyway

**Reverse osmosis.** Take a membrane so tight that water molecules can squeeze
through but hydrated ions cannot, then apply enough pressure to push water
across it against its natural tendency to flow the other way.

The scale involved is genuinely hard to picture. RO membranes operate around
**0.0001 microns** — sub-nanometre, tighter than 1.5 nm. Not a hole so much as
a molecular obstacle course that water can navigate and ions cannot.

That takes real force. Manual devices run at 5.5 MPa — around 800 psi,
generated by a person working a lever. This is why hand desalinators are hard
work and why they cost what they cost: you are hand-cranking a pressure vessel.

*(You will see "0.00001 microns" quoted in a lot of survival articles. That is
off by a factor of ten and would be smaller than a single atom. The real figure
is impressive enough.)*

### 2. Leave the salt behind

**Distillation.** Salt does not evaporate at the temperatures water does. Heat
seawater and pure water leaves as vapour while the salt stays put as crust.
Catch and cool the vapour and you have water purer than RO output — free not
only of salt but of most pathogens and heavy metals too.

The physics is free, foolproof, and requires no equipment. The catch is
throughput, and the catch is brutal.

## The four systems that actually work

### Manual reverse osmosis

Handheld mechanical pumps built for life rafts and ditch bags.

The **QuenchSea 3.0** ($400) uses a hand lever with an energy recovery device to
produce 5–8 litres per hour. No batteries, no fuel. At 6 kg it is heavy for a
grab bag, but nothing else in this price range approaches its output.

The **Katadyn Survivor 06** (~$1,000–1,700) is the orthodox choice — military
issue, decades of service, and just 1.13 kg. It produces **0.89 litres per
hour.** That gap is the entire trade: five times lighter, roughly seven times
slower.

### Forward osmosis pouches

The clever one. A multi-chamber pouch holds a membrane between raw seawater and
a concentrated sugar syrup. Because the syrup is *more* concentrated than
seawater, osmosis runs in its natural direction — water is pulled across the
membrane on its own, with no pump and no power. Roughly five hours later you
have a sweet, electrolyte-rich drink.

Its unique value is the case no pump covers: **when you physically cannot
pump.** Injury, exhaustion, hypothermia, or a crewmate who needs water while
you sail the boat. Its limit is equally absolute — every use consumes a syrup
charge, and when the charges are gone the pouch is dead weight.

Availability has collapsed. SeaPack is out of production and its maker, HTI, has
closed. The remaining option, the OTS Osmo Seal, carries far thinner
documentation.

### Solar and thermal distillation

The method everyone teaches: a cup inside a bowl of seawater, clear plastic over
the top, a stone in the centre to make a cone, set in the sun. Vapour condenses
on the plastic and drips into the cup.

It costs nothing and needs no gear. It also **will not keep you alive.**
Purpose-built stills manage 0.3–1.0 litres per square metre per sunny day.
An improvised bowl still is a fraction of a square metre — a few hundred
millilitres a day at best, against a human requirement of about three litres.

Learn it. It is free and it slows dehydration. Never plan around it.

Fire distillation — boiling with an angled lid channelling steam into a clean
bottle — is faster and works at night, but trades sunlight for fuel, and fuel
is usually the scarcer resource.

### Electric watermakers

The same RO principle with a motor instead of your arm, for cruising yachts and
off-grid coastal homes. Output runs from 6 to 65+ litres per hour, at $1,200 to
$7,000+.

Removing the human from the pressure loop removes the stamina ceiling — and
replaces it with a power budget. A 65 L/hr unit draws around 290 watts while
running. The question stops being "how strong am I" and becomes "how many
amp-hours can I spare."

## The specification nobody quotes

Every product page leads with salt rejection: 98%, 98.4%, 97%. It is the least
useful number in the category, because they are all fine.

Here is the number that decides things. **An adult needs about three litres of
water a day.**

| To make one person's daily water | Takes |
|---|---|
| Electric watermaker (30 L/hr) | 6 minutes |
| QuenchSea 3.0 | 25–35 minutes of pumping |
| Katadyn Survivor 06 | **~3.4 hours of continuous pumping** |
| Improvised solar still | Doesn't get there |

Three and a half hours of hard physical work, per person, per day, while
dehydrated and probably injured. The manual devices are not slower watermakers.
They are a different kind of object, and that number — not salt rejection — is
what you are actually buying.

## What to take from this

- **No filter desalinates.** Not one, at any price. Salt is dissolved, not
  suspended.
- **Only pressure or heat works.** Reverse osmosis or distillation. There is no
  third option.
- **Rate matters more than purity.** They all reject salt adequately. They do
  not all produce water fast enough to matter.
- **Layer the failures.** A pump fails when you are injured. A pouch fails when
  the syrup runs out. A still fails when there is no sun. A watermaker fails
  when the power dies. Pick a combination whose failure modes don't overlap.
- **Test it before you need it.** A $40 TDS pen is the only way to know a
  membrane still works. Untested gear is an assumption, not equipment.
