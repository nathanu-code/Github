# Choosing a Desalinator: Match the Failure Mode, Not the Spec Sheet

*Buyer-facing article. Data from [`products/`](../products/).*

---

Most desalinator comparisons line up salt rejection percentages and let you
pick the highest. That sorts the products in an order that means nothing —
98%, 98.4%, and 97% are all adequate, and none of them tell you whether the
device will produce water on the day you need it.

A better question: **what makes each one stop working, and can you survive
that?**

## Every option has one hard limit

| Option | Stops working when |
|---|---|
| Manual RO pump | You are too injured or exhausted to pump |
| Forward osmosis pouch | The syrup charges run out |
| Solar still | The sun goes down, or you have no area |
| Fire distillation | The fuel runs out |
| Electric watermaker | The power dies |

Read that column again and the shopping problem inverts. You are not buying
litres per hour. You are buying **a set of failure conditions**, and the goal
is a kit whose failures do not all happen on the same bad day.

A single electric watermaker on a boat is a good supply and a bad plan: the
storm that floods your electrics is exactly when you need water. A hand pump
alone is better, until the same storm breaks your arm.

## The two manual pumps are not competitors

They look like the same product at different prices. They are not.

| | Katadyn Survivor 06 | QuenchSea 3.0 |
|---|---|---|
| Output | 0.89 L/hr | 5–8 L/hr |
| Weight | 1.13 kg | 6 kg |
| Price | ~$1,000–1,700 | $400 |
| Warranty | — | 3 months, consumables excluded |
| Track record | Decades, military issue | Newer entrant |

The Survivor 06 costs two to four times more for one-seventh the output. That
sounds indefensible until you weigh the bags. **1.13 kg versus 6 kg** is the
difference between a device that lives in your ditch bag permanently and one
you have to decide to carry.

And the device you didn't carry has an output of zero.

- **Choose the Survivor 06** when weight decides — a life raft, a ditch bag, a
  solo kit, anything you grab while the boat sinks. It is what life-raft
  servicing expects and it has decades of field history.
- **Choose QuenchSea** when volume decides and 6 kg is affordable — a boat
  locker rather than a grab bag, a shore position, a group of three or more.
  At 5–8 L/hr it hydrates a crew; at 0.89 L/hr the Katadyn rations one person.
- **Choose both** if you are actually offshore. They are cheap relative to the
  boat and they cover each other.

## Where the pouches earn their place

Forward osmosis pouches look like the weakest option on paper: five hours per
use, capped by consumables, output is sugary rather than pure, and the best
product in the category is discontinued.

They cover one case nothing else does. **They require nothing from you.** No
pumping, no power, no sun, no fuel — you fill it, inject the syrup charge, and
wait while osmosis does the work.

That is the injured-crew case, and it is not hypothetical. The scenarios where
you most need water are the ones most likely to leave someone unable to work a
lever for three hours. An electrolyte drink for a dehydrated casualty is also
better than plain water.

Buy them as a **supplement to a pump, never instead of one** — and verify
availability and syrup expiry, because HTI's closure has left this category
thin.

## Be honest about the solar still

Learn the method. It is free, it needs no gear, and knowing it costs you
nothing.

Then look at the numbers: 0.3–1.0 L per square metre per sunny day for a
purpose-built still, against a human need of ~3 L/day. The improvised
bowl-and-plastic version is a fraction of a square metre. It produces a few
hundred millilitres on a good day.

Solar distillation is a real technology at real scale — a 5 m² installation can
water a family, and for a static coastal position with footprint and sun it
deserves serious consideration. On a raft, where you have neither area nor
time, it is a comfort, not a supply.

**The improvised still is a technique, not a plan.** Any guide that presents it
as your seawater answer is selling reassurance.

## Recommended kits by use case

**Life raft / ditch bag.** Katadyn Survivor 06 as primary. Add forward osmosis
pouches if available, sized against realistic days adrift — remember four
pouches is roughly one person-day. Weight rules everything here.

**Cruising yacht.** Electric watermaker sized to your amp-hours (30 L/hr at
11 A @ 12 V is the practical sweet spot) for daily living, plus a manual pump
stowed in the raft. Two layers, two independent failure modes. Non-negotiable
offshore.

**Coastal home / bug-out position.** Petrol or electric watermaker as supply, a
manual pump as the power-failure backup, and a purpose-built solar still if you
have 5 m² to spare. This is the one context where distillation genuinely
contributes.

**Coastal camping / hiking.** QuenchSea if 6 kg is acceptable and you want real
volume; Survivor 06 if grams matter more than litres. Know the still method
either way.

## Before you buy

1. **Confirm the part number.** Price spreads across retailers for nominally
   the same Katadyn family run 40%+.
2. **Read the warranty on the cheap end.** QuenchSea's is three months and
   excludes membranes, pre-filters, and seals — the parts that fail first.
3. **Price the consumables.** Membranes and pre-filters are recurring. QuenchSea
   doesn't publish lifespans, so total cost of ownership can't be modelled from
   published data — ask before buying.
4. **Check production status.** Several Katadyn/Spectra models are being
   discontinued or rebranded; SeaPack is gone entirely.
5. **Buy a TDS pen (~$40) and test on arrival.** It is the only way to know the
   membrane works. Then test again annually.

## The short version

Salt rejection is a tiebreaker, not a decision. Choose on **weight if it lives
in a grab bag, volume if it lives in a locker, and amp-hours if it lives on a
boat** — then add a second option that fails for a different reason than the
first.
