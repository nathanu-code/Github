## 1. The filter myth (hook: counterintuitive)

Your survival straw does nothing against seawater.

Not "works poorly." Nothing.

Filters work when the bad thing is bigger than water. Salt isn't. It dissolves
into individual ions that wrap themselves in water molecules — a mesh fine
enough to stop them stops the water too.

There is no gap to exploit. There's nothing to strain.

Only two things separate salt from water:
→ Reverse osmosis (force water through a 0.0001-micron membrane)
→ Distillation (evaporate the water, leave the salt)

That's the entire list. At any price.

## 3. Solar still reality check (hook: myth-busting)

The bowl-and-plastic-wrap solar still is in every survival guide.

It will not keep you alive.

Purpose-built stills: 0.3–1.0 L per square metre per sunny day.
An improvised bowl still: a fraction of a square metre.
You need: ~3 L/day.

Do the arithmetic and you get a few hundred millilitres. On a good day.

Learn it anyway — it's free and it slows dehydration. Just never plan around
it. A 5 m² purpose-built still can water a family. A cereal bowl can't water
you.

Technique, not a plan.

## 5. The clever one (hook: mechanism)

There's a desalinator that needs no pump, no power, no sun, and no fuel.

A pouch with a membrane between seawater and concentrated sugar syrup. Because
the syrup is *more* concentrated than seawater, osmosis runs in its natural
direction — water gets pulled across on its own. Five hours later: a sweet,
electrolyte-rich drink.

Why it matters: it's the only option that asks nothing of you.

The scenarios where you most need water are the ones most likely to leave
someone unable to work a lever for three hours. Injury. Exhaustion.
Hypothermia.

Catch: every use burns a syrup charge. Then it's dead weight.
Bigger catch: SeaPack's maker closed. This category is thin now.

Supplement to a pump. Never instead of one.

## 6. Failure modes (hook: framework)

Stop comparing desalinators on specs. Compare them on what kills them.

Manual pump → you're too injured to pump
FO pouch → syrup charges run out
Solar still → sun goes down
Fire still → fuel runs out
Electric watermaker → power dies

Now the shopping problem inverts. You're not buying litres per hour. You're
buying a set of failure conditions.

The goal: a kit whose failures don't all happen on the same bad day.

One watermaker on a boat is a good supply and a bad plan — the storm that
floods your electrics is exactly when you need water.
