# Hydroponics vs Aquaponics — Which Is Harder

Captured 2026-09-08. Sources: [Clemson HGIC — aquaponics vs hydroponics](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/aquaponics-vs-hydroponics-which-is-best/),
[Upstart University — comparison](https://university.upstartfarmers.com/blog/aquaponics-vs-hydroponics-which-is-better),
[Atlas Scientific — picking an approach](https://atlas-scientific.com/blog/aquaponics-vs-hydroponics/),
[UH CTAHR — three non-circulating hydroponic methods for lettuce (PDF)](https://www.ctahr.hawaii.edu/hawaii/downloads/three_non-circulating_hydroponic_methods_for_growing_lettuce.pdf),
[Master Gardener San Diego — Kratky method](https://www.mastergardenersd.org/hydroponic-gardening-with-the-kratky-method-ideal-for-small-spaces/),
[Scale & Stem — Kratky with zero electricity](https://scaleandstem.com/articles/kratky-method/)

## The answer

**Aquaponics is clearly harder.** You are "running an aquatic farm and a garden
at once" — three living systems (fish, nitrifying bacteria, plants) instead of
one.

But there's a third option that beats both on difficulty, and it happens to
solve the power-fragility problem that made aquaponics a poor preparedness bet.

## Difficulty ranking

| Rank | Method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — easiest** | **Kratky (passive hydro)** | **No pump, no power, no moving parts** |
| 2 | Pumped hydroponics (DWC, NFT) | One system to manage, full nutrient control |
| 3 — hardest | Aquaponics | Three organisms, 6–8 week startup, compromised pH |

## Head-to-head

| | Hydroponics | Aquaponics |
|---|---|---|
| **Startup time** | **A couple of days** to stabilise the solution | **6–8 weeks** fishless cycling before planting |
| **pH target** | **5.5–6.5 — the plant optimum** | 6.8–7.2 — a compromise, and **iron locks out above 7.0** |
| **Daily monitoring** | pH + EC (1.2–2.5) | pH + **ammonia (<0.5 ppm)** — a toxicity check, not just a nutrition one |
| **Nutrients** | Bought salts, precisely dosed | Fish feed via bacteria, **plus supplements** |
| **Fruiting crops** | **Straightforward** — dial up K, P, Ca, Fe | **Difficult** — feed can't supply enough |
| **Recovery from error** | Dump and refill | **Can't** — dumping kills the fish |
| **Protein output** | None | Fish |
| **Living things to kill** | Plants | **Fish, bacteria, and plants** |

**That 6–8 weeks is the underrated cost.** You cannot plant into a new
aquaponics system — the biofilter has to mature first. Hydroponics grows food
in week one.

And here's the part that undercuts the usual pitch: **during that 4–8 week
cycling period you are feeding fish *and* dosing partial hydroponic nutrients
anyway** to keep plants alive. You run a hydroponic system regardless — you just
also have fish to worry about.

Warm water shortens cycling by up to two weeks, which matters if you're in a
tropical zone.

## Hydroponic food is genuinely easier to grow well

This is the practical upshot for what you can actually harvest. Because you set
EC and pH independently and dose whatever's missing:

- **You run at pH 5.5–6.5**, the plant optimum — so **no iron lockout**, and
  none of the [chelated-iron firefighting](crop-selection.md) aquaponics demands.
- **Fruiting crops work.** Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers and strawberries need
  more potassium and phosphorus than fish feed supplies. In hydroponics you just
  add more. This is the single biggest capability difference.
- **Deficiencies are fixable in an afternoon**, not a multi-week rebalancing of
  a living system.

The trade-off: hydroponics needs **less setup effort but more ongoing
re-balancing** through the growing cycle, and the solution needs periodic
replacement.

## Kratky — the one that needs no power

Developed in 2004 by **Bernard Kratky, PhD, at the University of Hawaii.**
Plants sit in net pots in a lid, roots suspended in nutrient solution. As the
plant drinks, the water level drops and the upper roots end up in an **air gap**,
taking oxygen directly from the air.

The plant grows **two root systems at once — one for water, one for breathing** —
and the whole thing "runs on gravity and plant biology alone."

| Spec | Value |
|---|---|
| Power | **None** |
| Pumps, airstones, moving parts | **None** |
| Solution per 200 g lettuce (measured in Hilo) | **3–4 litres** |
| Loose-leaf lettuce | **30–35 days** |
| Full heads | 45–55 days |

**Three to four litres of water for a full head of lettuce, with no
electricity.** That is a remarkable number, and it's why this matters for the
resilience question.

### Kratky's real limitations

1. **One crop per fill.** You set it up, it runs down, you harvest and reset.
   Topping up mid-cycle defeats the air gap the roots depend on.
2. **Don't mix drinking rates in one reservoir.** A documented failure: put
   thirsty bok choy in with butterhead lettuce and the bok choy outpaces it,
   leaving the lettuce short of water and nutrients — "big healthy bok choy and
   stunted lettuce." One crop type per container.
3. **Leafy greens and herbs only.** Long-season and fruiting crops outlast the
   reservoir.
4. **Cover the reservoir.** Open standing water breeds mosquitoes. This is a
   real hygiene issue, not a footnote — especially in a warm climate.

## Failure modes — the comparison that actually matters

| Method | Fails when | Time to disaster |
|---|---|---|
| **Kratky** | You forget to reset it | **Days to weeks** |
| DWC (deep water culture) | Pump/air fails — water reserve buffers, but oxygen crashes | Many hours |
| **NFT (nutrient film)** | Pump fails — a thin film, **no reserve**, roots dry out | **Hours** |
| **Aquaponics** | Aeration fails — fish suffocate, **then the biofilter crashes and spikes ammonia after power returns** | **Hours, with a second failure behind it** |

Also worth knowing for any recirculating system: **root disease (Pythium, root
rot) spreads through shared solution fast.** Kratky's isolated containers are
structurally immune to that — another quiet advantage.

## Neither one is a closed loop

The "aquaponics is self-sufficient" claim doesn't survive contact with the
numbers. **Both systems have a permanent consumable dependency** — the loop is
open at the feed inlet:

| | Consumable | Storability |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydroponics** | Nutrient salts | **Concentrated** — a small bag makes hundreds of gallons |
| **Aquaponics** | Fish feed **+ K, Ca, Fe supplements** | **Bulky** — fish food "is used in greater amounts" |

So aquaponics swaps nutrient salts for fish feed, and then **still needs the
supplements anyway.**

**The counterintuitive consequence: hydroponic nutrients stockpile better than
fish feed.** They're concentrated and shelf-stable; fish feed is bulky, and
it degrades. If the reason you're interested is running without resupply, that
inverts the usual conclusion.

## Recommendation

**For food, easily, and now: pumped hydroponics.** Growing in week one instead
of week eight, pH at the plant optimum, fruiting crops actually viable, and
mistakes are recoverable by dumping the tank.

**For resilience: Kratky, without much competition.** It's the only method here
that doesn't die when the power does — and given that
[power fragility is aquaponics' fatal flaw](aquaponics.md), that settles it.
Pair it with [sprouts in a jar](seeds-and-propagation.md) and you have a
fresh-food capability with **no electrical failure mode at all.**

**Aquaponics only if you specifically want the fish.** The protein is the reason
to accept three organisms, a 6–8 week startup, a compromised pH, and a system
you can't reset. If you don't want fish, it's strictly harder for no gain.

If you're in Hawaii, note that Kratky was developed at UH with the Hilo lettuce
data above — it was designed for exactly that climate, and year-round warmth
means no reservoir heating and continuous cycling.
