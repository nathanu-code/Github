## Legality

**Rainwater harvesting is legal in all 50 US states.** Some states impose rules
on storage capacity, permitted uses, or require registration — check your state
and municipality before building at scale, particularly for potable use.

## System architecture

Five components, in order:

1. **Catchment** — the roof. Area and material both matter.
2. **Conveyance** — gutters and downspouts, PVC or metal.
3. **Pre-treatment accessories** — leaf eater / screen, and a **first flush
   diverter**.
4. **Storage** — tanks or IBC totes.
5. **Distribution** — pump, hoses, and filtration/UV if the water is for
   drinking.

## The first flush diverter is the part people skip

The first 1–2 gallons off a roof carries the worst of what has accumulated
there: bird droppings, dust, pollen, roof grit, insects. A first flush diverter
captures that initial volume and sends it to waste; once full, it directs the
cleaner water into storage.

- **Commercial units: $20–60.**
- **DIY:** a vertical standpipe of PVC with a small ball valve at the bottom
  that drains slowly between rain events. Functionally equivalent.

For $20–60 this is the single highest-leverage water-quality component in the
whole system. Fit one.

## Storage — IBC tote cautions

IBC totes (typically 275 gal) are the cheap route to volume, but:

- **Use food-grade totes only.**
- **Do not use reconditioned totes** that previously held chemicals. "Cleaned"
  is not good enough for water you intend to drink.
- **Block light.** Clear or translucent totes grow algae fast. Paint them, wrap
  them, or site them in shade.
- **Support the weight.** Water is ~8.34 lb/gal, so a full 275 gal tote is over
  **2,300 lb**. It needs a level, load-rated base — gravel pad, pavers, or
  timber cribbing.
- **Screen every opening** against mosquitoes.

## Yield estimate

Roughly **0.6 gallons per square foot of roof per inch of rain** (after
accounting for losses). So 1 inch of rain on a 1,000 sq ft roof yields
approximately **600 gallons**.

Run that against your own rainfall and roof area before sizing storage — most
people over-build storage and under-build conveyance, when the binding
constraint is usually how much of a downpour the gutters can actually capture.

## Roof material matters for potable use

Metal roofs are the preferred catchment surface. Avoid water from asphalt
shingle, treated wood shake, or lead-flashed roofs for drinking without serious
treatment — these shed particulates and chemicals that filtration may not fully
address.

## Rainwater is not automatically safe to drink

Rainwater is clean when it leaves the sky and contaminated by the time it
reaches your tank. Treat it as surface water:

1. **First flush diversion** — removes the worst load.
2. **Sediment filtration** — pre-filter to protect downstream elements.
3. **Microbiological filtration** — Berkey-class gravity filter or equivalent,
   for bacteria, protozoa and viruses.
4. **Disinfection** — UV, or chlorine/boiling, for potable use.

Note that rainwater is naturally very **low in dissolved minerals** — like RO
output, it is flat-tasting and mineral-poor. Fine short-term; over months as a
sole source it wants mineral or electrolyte supplementation.

## Sourcing note

This is the **safest category to source generically.** Tanks, gutters,
downspouts, fittings, screens, first flush hardware, and pumps are commodities;
the failure mode is a leak, not a fire or a poisoning. The one exception is
anything in contact with drinking water — insist on **food-grade / NSF-rated**
plastics and confirm it, since that claim is cheap to make and consequential to
get wrong.
