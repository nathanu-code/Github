## The direct answer

**Propane, in 20 lb refillable tanks.** It wins on every axis you asked about
simultaneously — and that is unusual enough to make this an easy call.

| Criterion | Propane verdict |
|---|---|
| Shelf life | **Indefinite.** The steel tank is the limiting part, and galvanized tanks last 30+ years |
| Cost per BTU | **Cheapest.** ~$1.30/lb refill; 1 lb camping canisters cost 6–10× more per gallon |
| Cold weather | **Works anywhere.** Boiling point −44 °F |
| Availability | Refillable at any hardware store, gas station, or U-Haul |
| Appliance choice | Enormous — stoves, heaters, lanterns, generators, water heaters |

Nothing else in the category is close. Buy propane.

## Fuel shelf life — the full picture

| Fuel | Shelf life | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Propane** | **Indefinite** | Tank integrity is the limit, not the fuel |
| **Alcohol** (denatured/ethanol) | **Indefinite** | Simple, safe, low output |
| **Butane** canisters | ~8 yrs mfr-recommended; near-indefinite sealed | Cool, dry storage |
| **Kerosene** | ~5 yrs | Extendable with annual stabiliser |
| **White gas** (Coleman fuel) | 5–7 yrs sealed / **~2 yrs opened** | Big penalty once opened |
| **Gasoline** | **3–6 months** (ethanol blend); 12 mo absolute max | Ethanol pulls water from the air |
| **Wood** | n/a | Renewable — never expires, never runs out |

**Gasoline is the trap.** It is the fuel most people already have and the one
that spoils fastest. A stockpile built on gasoline needs continuous rotation.
Do not plan around it.

## Cold weather is where cheap gas fails

This is the failure mode that catches people, because it is invisible at
purchase and absolute in the field.

| Fuel | Stops vaporizing below |
|---|---|
| Propane | −44 °F |
| Isobutane | ~11 °F |
| n-Butane | **~30 °F** |

Practical guidance from the field: butane stoves work best **above 50 °F**;
isobutane blends are reliable to roughly **15 °F**.

There is a second-order trap. Canister stoves run a *blend* of propane,
isobutane and sometimes n-butane. In the cold the propane fraction burns off
disproportionately, so canister pressure and performance **degrade as the
canister empties** — a half-used canister performs worse than a fresh one at the
same temperature.

Pure propane isn't sold in lightweight canisters because it needs much higher
storage pressure, which requires heavier steel. For a stockpile, where weight
doesn't matter, that trade-off disappears entirely — which is exactly why 20 lb
tanks are the answer at home and canisters are the answer in a backpack.

## Cost comparison

| Option | Cost | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|
| 20 lb propane tank | ~$35 tank + $26–40 refill | ~$1.30/lb |
| 1 lb propane cylinder | $2.50–4.50 ea | 6–10× bulk propane per gallon |
| 1 lb butane canister | $2.50–4.50 ea | ≥2× a 16.4 oz propane cylinder per BTU |

Refillable 1 lb cylinders exist and pay back quickly if you want canister
convenience at bulk-propane cost. Disposables are for occasional use, not
stockpiling.

## Stove recommendations by layer

Apply the same failure-mode logic as the desalination guide: buy a set whose
failure conditions do not overlap.

### Layer 1 — Base camp / home (propane)

Any propane burner plus 20 lb tanks. A two-burner Coleman-class stove or a
cast-iron high-output burner. Cheap, indestructible, and your fuel never
expires. **This is where 90% of your cooking should happen.**

### Layer 2 — Multi-fuel liquid (burns whatever exists)

For the case where you're out of propane and running on whatever you can find:

| Stove | Fuels | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **MSR WhisperLite Universal** | Canister gas + white gas + kerosene | Lighter and quieter; simmers usably |
| **Primus OmniFuel** | Same range | **Best fuel switching** — clearly better than the MSR at changing over |
| **MSR XGK EX** | Liquid fuels | Expedition-grade: most heat, best wind resistance, poor simmer |

If you want one stove that burns anything, the OmniFuel's switching advantage is
the differentiator. If weight matters more, the WhisperLite Universal. If you
want maximum output in bad conditions and don't care about simmering, the XGK.

### Layer 3 — Wood (fuel that never runs out)

A rocket stove or wood gasifier stove. The fuel is free, unlimited, and cannot
expire. Slower, smokier, and it signals your position — but it is the only layer
with no supply chain at all. Every kit should have one.

### Layer 4 — Alcohol (simplest possible)

No moving parts, nothing to fail, indefinite fuel shelf life. Low output and
slow. Worth having as the backup that cannot break.

## What to stockpile

1. **Propane, as much as you can safely store.** Multiple 20 lb tanks. Store
   outdoors or in a detached, ventilated space — never in a basement or
   living area.
2. **A wood-burning option.** Removes the supply chain from the equation.
3. **Denatured alcohol** as a compact, indefinite-shelf-life reserve.
4. **Skip gasoline** for cooking-fuel purposes. If you keep it for vehicles,
   rotate it and treat it with stabiliser.

Note the difference between *stockpiling* and *carrying*. Propane wins for
storage; for a grab bag, isobutane canisters or a multi-fuel liquid stove win on
weight. These are separate purchases for separate jobs.

## How the Alibaba stove fits

The [Vooma remote-canister stove](alibaba-vooma-camping-stove.md) at $18–23 is a
reasonable car-camping unit, but it is **not a stockpiling answer**: a butane or
proprietary-canister system is precisely the fuel profile to avoid — worst cold
performance, highest cost per BTU, and dependence on a canister standard that
may not be locally available.

Buy it, if you buy it, as convenience gear. Build the actual fuel plan on
propane.
