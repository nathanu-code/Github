## Do you need seeds? Yes — and there's a trap

Hydroponics and aquaponics don't change plant biology. You need seeds, or you
need another way to make new plants.

**The trap: nearly every variety bred for hydroponics is an F1 hybrid.** F1 seed
**segregates** in the next generation — saved seed produces offspring that "vary
wildly in size, flavor, yield, disease resistance and more, typically for the
worse." Commercial growers want F1 precisely *because* of uniformity, which is
what controlled-environment production is built on.

So a standard hydroponic setup has **zero seed independence.** If running
without resupply matters to you, you have to buy **open-pollinated or heirloom**
deliberately, from day one. It is not something you can switch to later using
the seed you have.

| Seed type | Saves true? | Uniformity | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open-pollinated / heirloom** | **Yes** | Variable | The only option if you need seed independence |
| **F1 hybrid** | **No** — segregates | Excellent | What almost all hydro varieties are |

## Seed saving inside a hydro rack is expensive

It works, but the cost is space-time under lights:

- You must let the plant **bolt** — lettuce goes tall and bitter and occupies
  prime lit rack space for **months past harvest** for one seed head.
- In a system sized by feed rate ratio (see [`aquaponics.md`](aquaponics.md))
  and paying for lighting, that's real money per seed.
- **Lettuce is the easiest** starting point: it self-pollinates and needs little
  or no isolation. One caution — if two varieties bolt side by side, remove one
  to prevent crossing.
- **Biennials are effectively impossible** indoors: carrots, beets, onions, and
  cabbage need two seasons plus a cold period to set seed.

## The real answer: skip seeds entirely

This is the part most discussions miss. **Design around vegetative propagation
and the seed question largely disappears.**

| Crop | How it propagates | Seed needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Basil, mint, rosemary, oregano, thyme, sage | Cuttings root in plain water | **No** |
| Green onions / scallions | Regrow from the root base | **No** |
| Tomatoes, peppers | Clone from cuttings | **No** |
| Sweet potato | Slips sprouted from a tuber | **No** |
| Potatoes | Seed potatoes (tubers) | **No** |
| Strawberries | Runners | **No** |
| Ginger, turmeric | Rhizome division | **No** |
| Lettuce | Cut-and-come-again — several harvests per plant | Reduces need |

**A single basil mother plant supplies cuttings indefinitely.** Herbs are also
the highest-value-per-square-foot crop in the system, so this is not a
compromise — it's the better design anyway.

Build the system around **perennial herbs propagated by cuttings**, keep
open-pollinated lettuce seed as your annual, and the resupply problem shrinks to
almost nothing.

## Seed storage — the one rule to remember

**Temperature (°F) + relative humidity should stay under 100.**

So 40 °F at 50% RH = 90 → good. 70 °F at 60% RH = 130 → poor. Airtight
container with a desiccant packet, in the fridge, is the practical answer.

| Crop | Viability, well stored |
|---|---|
| Tomato | 4–10 years |
| Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, kale) | 4–5 years |
| **Lettuce** | **3–6 years** |
| Beans, peas | 3–4 years |
| Onion, parsnip | **1–2 years only** |

Rotate the short-lived ones; the long-lived ones can sit for most of a decade.

## The best preparedness answer isn't hydroponics at all

**Your food storage is already a seed bank.**

Stored wheat, lentils, mung beans, alfalfa, radish, broccoli, sunflower, and
peas can be **sprouted into fresh food in 3–7 days** — with **no lights, no
pumps, no power, and no system**. Microgreens take 7–14 days with minimal light.

| Method | Time to food | Power needed | Equipment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sprouts** | **3–7 days** | **None** | A jar |
| Microgreens | 7–14 days | Minimal light | A tray |
| Aquaponics greens | ~28 days | **Continuous** — fish die in hours without it | Full system |
| Soil garden | 30–100 days | None | Ground |

Sprouting is the **most power-resilient fresh-food method there is**, it runs on
commodity seed that stores for years, and it costs almost nothing to set up.
Against the [aquaponics power-fragility problem](aquaponics.md), it's the
stronger play for resilience — and the two aren't exclusive.

**Practical recommendation:** buy a few pounds of open-pollinated sprouting seed
(alfalfa, radish, broccoli, mung bean) and store it under the °F+RH rule. It's
the cheapest fresh-food insurance in this whole set of records, and unlike every
other option here it has no failure mode involving electricity.
