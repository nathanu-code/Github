## Mushrooms: what they're actually for

**They are not a protein source, and it's worth being blunt about that before
the how-to.**

Protein runs **16.8–27.6% of dry weight** — which sounds respectable until you
account for water. Fresh mushrooms are roughly 90% water. Run the arithmetic at
100% biological efficiency:

> 1 kg dry straw → 1 kg **fresh** mushrooms → ~100 g dry matter → **~25 g protein**

**About 25 grams of protein per kilogram of straw.** Nobody is living on that.

**What they are for** is the thing nothing else in these records does:
**they convert waste cellulose into food, in the dark, with no light and
essentially no power.** Straw, cardboard, coffee grounds, sawdust, spent
brewing grain — material that is otherwise compost — becomes food. Plus real
umami, texture, B vitamins, and vitamin D if you expose the harvested caps to
sunlight.

Judge them as a **flavour, micronutrient, and waste-conversion crop.** On those
terms they're excellent.

## Growing mushrooms — easiest first

| Species | Difficulty | Substrate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oyster** (*Pleurotus ostreatus*, *pulmonarius*) | **Easiest** | Straw, coffee grounds, cardboard, hardwood sawdust | "Very easy to cultivate on a large range of substrates." Aggressive coloniser that outcompetes contaminants — which is *why* it's the beginner choice |
| **Wine cap** (*Stropharia rugosoannulata*) | Easiest outdoors | Wood chip beds | Near-zero effort once established; just a bed in shade |
| **Shiitake** | Moderate | Hardwood logs or sawdust blocks | 6–12 months on logs, then **years** of production. Higher value |
| Lion's mane | Moderate | Hardwood sawdust | Sensitive to fresh-air exchange |
| Button / cremini (*Agaricus*) | **Hardest at home** | Composted manure | Needs proper composting — skip it |

### Yield and timing (oyster, verified)

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Biological efficiency | **58–136%** depending on substrate (wheat straw peaked at **136%**; maize straw 114%) |
| Full lifecycle | **24–31 days** (24 on alfalfa pulp, 31 on commercial straw) |
| Protein (dry weight) | 16.8% on wheat straw → **23.2% on faba bean straw** |

Two useful patterns in that data:

1. **Protein content tracks the nitrogen content of the substrate.** Legume
   straw gives higher-protein mushrooms than cereal straw. If you're growing
   for nutrition rather than volume, grow on bean straw.
2. **Faster substrates reduce contamination risk** — the 24-day alfalfa cycle
   beats 31-day straw partly because there's less time for competitors to
   establish.

### Method, in brief

Pasteurise straw (hot water, 65–80 °C, an hour), drain, mix with grain spawn,
pack into a bucket or grow bag with holes, colonise **in the dark** for 2–3
weeks, then fruit in high humidity with **fresh air exchange**. Poor air
exchange is the classic beginner failure — CO₂ buildup gives long stems and
tiny caps.

### You don't have to keep buying spawn

The same point as [vegetative propagation](seeds-and-propagation.md), applied to
fungi: **oyster mushrooms clone from stem butts on damp cardboard.** Save the
bases of what you harvest, and you have perpetual spawn. Grain-to-grain transfer
expands it faster.

So mushrooms join herbs, taro and sweet potato on the list of crops with **no
recurring input purchase.**

## The protein ladder — ranked for space and speed

### 1. Coturnix quail — the standout

| Metric | Quail | Chickens |
|---|---|---|
| **Space per bird** | **1 sq ft** | 8–10 sq ft |
| **Age at first egg** | **6–8 weeks** | ~20–24 weeks |
| Eggs per hen per year | **~300** | ~250–300 |
| Time to processing weight | **7–8 weeks** (150–200 g dressed) | 8–16 weeks |
| Feed conversion (Jumbo strain) | ~3:1 | ~2–3:1 |
| Meat | **~25% protein, ~4% fat, 126 kcal/100 g** | Lower protein, higher fat |

**Eight to ten times the space efficiency, and laying in six to eight weeks
instead of five months.** For a space-constrained setup that is not a close
call. Quail also win on startup cost and speed to production.

The catches: eggs are small (5 or so per chicken egg), quail are less hardy
individually, they don't brood reliably so you need an incubator, and some
municipalities regulate poultry — quail sometimes escape those rules as "game
birds," sometimes don't. Check locally.

### 2. Fish — covered in [`aquaponics.md`](aquaponics.md)

Roughly **2–3.5 lb of fillet per month** from a 300-gallon system, with fish
taking 6–9 months to reach harvest weight. Real protein, but slow and
power-fragile.

### 3. Rabbits

The strongest meat converter of the small livestock options: they breed fast,
are quiet, need little space, and can run largely on forage — which is the key
advantage, since they don't compete with you for grain the way poultry does.
Rabbit is very lean. Worth investigating if you have forage available; I don't
have verified conversion figures to quote here.

### 4. Chickens

Better eggs by size, hardier, will brood their own replacements, and eat kitchen
scraps happily. But **8–10 sq ft per bird and five months to first egg** is a
poor trade against quail unless you have the room.

### 5. Sprouted legumes and dry beans

No animals, no power, no permits. Dry beans store for years and
[sprout in 3–7 days](seeds-and-propagation.md). Lower protein per square foot of
garden than potatoes give calories, but they store longest and cover the protein
that root crops can't.

## The loop-closer: black soldier fly larvae

**This is the most useful thing in this record.** Earlier I flagged that
neither aquaponics nor hydroponics is a closed loop — you buy fish feed or
nutrient salts forever. **BSFL is the actual answer to that.**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Crude protein | **42.1%** (defatted meal: **56.9%**) |
| Range across substrates | 29.9–48.2% |
| Fat | ~35% |
| Substrate | **Food waste** — fruit, vegetable, and organic residues |
| Harvest | 14 days, just before pupation |

And the feeding trial result that matters:

> In Nile tilapia, **80 g/kg of BSF meal successfully substituted for 70 g/kg of
> fishmeal and 350 g/kg of soybean meal** with no adverse effect on growth rate
> or feed efficiency.

**Your food waste becomes your fish feed.** That converts the open inlet in the
aquaponics loop into a closed one, and it costs almost nothing.

### Why BSFL specifically, not other insects

- **They self-harvest.** Mature prepupae instinctively crawl out to find a
  pupation site. Build a ladder structure inside the bin and they climb it into
  a collection container **on their own** — no sorting, no sieving.
- **The adults are harmless.** Black soldier flies don't eat, don't bite, and
  aren't disease vectors — unlike houseflies. A BSFL colony actually
  *outcompetes* houseflies for the same waste.
- **No power required.** A bin, waste, warmth.
- **They eat what you can't compost easily** — meat scraps, dairy, oils.

Feed them to quail and fish alike. The cascade is genuinely elegant:

```
kitchen + garden waste → BSFL → quail + fish → protein
                       ↘ frass (castings) → garden beds
spent mushroom substrate → worms/BSFL → same loop
```

## Recommendation

**If you want protein, buy quail.** 1 sq ft per bird, eggs in 6–8 weeks, ~300
eggs a year, meat at 25% protein. Nothing else in this set produces protein that
fast in that little space, and it needs no electricity.

**If you want to close the feed loop, build a BSFL bin.** It's nearly free, runs
on waste, self-harvests, needs no power, and substitutes for both fishmeal and
soybean meal in tilapia feed. It's the single highest-leverage thing in this
record.

**Grow oyster mushrooms because they're easy and delicious**, not because
they're protein. 24–31 days, 58–136% biological efficiency on free straw, in the
dark, no power — and cloneable from stem butts so you never buy spawn again.

**Skip chickens** unless you have the space; quail dominate on every metric that
matters at small scale.

## If you're in Hawaii

Two of these get materially better:

1. **BSF breeds year-round in the tropics.** In temperate zones colonies stall
   over winter and have to be restarted; in Hawaii the colony runs continuously.
   Black soldier flies are already well established there, so seeding a bin is
   often just a matter of leaving it open.
2. **Oyster mushrooms suit the climate.** The warm-tolerant species
   (*P. pulmonarius*, *P. djamor*) fruit well in heat and humidity, where
   temperate growers fight to maintain both.

Combined with year-round tilapia and no reservoir heating, that makes the
**BSFL → quail + fish** loop considerably stronger there than the temperate
numbers above suggest.
