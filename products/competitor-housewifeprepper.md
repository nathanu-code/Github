## The breakdown

### 1. The prep content is the hook; wellness is the revenue

Count the mix: **11 preparedness items against 15 wellness/supplement/food
items.** The "prepper" identity acquires the audience, but the offer sheet
leans toward supplements, red-light devices, and saunas.

That's almost certainly deliberate, and it's the commission structure talking:

| Category | Typical affiliate economics |
|---|---|
| Hardware (Jackery, generators) | Low single-digit % on a low-margin box |
| Supplements & consumables | **High %, and they repeat** |
| $2,000–5,000 saunas / red-light | **High % on a large ticket** |

**Prep hardware doesn't pay well enough to build a business on.** She monetizes
the audience with high-margin recurring wellness products instead. If you're
weighing a similar play, that is the single most useful thing on the page.

### 2. Twenty-plus separate affiliate programs, one coupon word

She's running BON CHARGE, AquaTru, Manukora, Eyeganics, LumeBox, Danger Coffee,
Sunlighten, Carnivore Bar, Kono, TWC, MOS, Masa, Rockwell, Faraday Defense,
AirDoctor, Therasage, Jackery, Lectric, DNA Company, Sperti, Aussie Trace
Minerals, Jase Medical, Firman, Amble — plus Amazon and ShopMy.

**And nearly every code is the same word: `HOUSEWIFE`.** One memorable token
across two dozen programs. Easy to say on camera, easy for viewers to recall,
and each merchant tracks it independently. That's a well-executed detail worth
copying structurally.

### 4. Categories where the marketing outruns the evidence

Flagging these because you asked about selling in adjacent space, and because
[label claims carry real exposure](selling-kratky-and-fresh-produce.md):

- **"Spike Support" / "Long Haul Formula"** — marketed around post-vaccine
  "spike protein detox." That is not an established medical intervention.
- **EMP TRAP / DEFCON car device** — EMP-protection claims are effectively
  unverifiable by a buyer. Faraday bags themselves are testable and legitimate;
  whole-vehicle "EMP protection" is not.
- **EMF-blocking phone and home products** — the health premise is not
  supported by mainstream evidence.
- **Ozone bubbler** — ozone is not an approved therapy for internal use, and
  inhaled ozone is a respiratory irritant.
- **Genetic test for health optimization** — interpretive claims vary widely
  between providers.

None of that is a comment on her; it's a note that **these categories sell well
in this niche precisely because the claims are strong**, and that health claims
draw FTC and FDA attention in a way that "pesticide-free lettuce" does not. If
you build in this space, the hard-goods half of her list is the defensible
half.

### 5. The structural template

Worth copying regardless of product choice:

1. **One link-in-bio page on a custom domain** — `housewifeprepper.bio` rather
   than a raw `beacons.ai/...` URL. Looks like a brand, and it survived the
   Cloudflare block that killed the beacons.ai URL.
2. **Three funnels into deeper catalogs** — Amazon storefront, ShopMy, and a
   "current best sellers" link. The bio page is the index, not the inventory.
3. **Offers ordered by what's converting**, not by category — best sellers and
   the 30%-off generator sit near the top.
4. **One coupon word everywhere.**
5. **Press links as social proof**, placed below the offers.
6. **Cross-posted to IG, TikTok, and Facebook**, all pointing at the one page.

## What I'd take from this

**Copy the structure, not the links.** The affiliate URLs are hers, and the
wellness half of the catalog carries claim risk you don't need.

The transferable lessons: **prep hardware alone won't fund a business** —
which is consistent with the [thin hardware margins](reselling-economics.md)
already documented — and **one memorable coupon word across many programs** is
free leverage.

If you want the rest of her catalog, the Amazon storefront and ShopMy
collections need a manual look — both blocked automated access.
