## Short answer

**Don't sell Kratky hardware. Sell the consumables, the seedlings, or the food.**

**To be clear about what this does and doesn't say:** Kratky is an excellent way
to *grow* — see [`hydroponics-vs-aquaponics.md`](hydroponics-vs-aquaponics.md)
for why it's the easiest method here and the only one with no electrical failure
mode. It is a bad thing to *manufacture and ship*. Those aren't in tension: it's
unsellable **because** it's cheap and simple to build, which is the same reason
it's worth building. Use it as your production method; don't put buckets in a
shipping container.

A Kratky system *is* a food-grade bucket, a lid with holes, and net pots —
**$10–15 in materials**, and the instructions are free and public (the
University of Hawaii CTAHR publication). The simplicity that makes Kratky good
is exactly what makes it unsellable as a manufactured product.

And importing them would repeat the mistake from the
[grow tent analysis](grow-tents-and-vertical-farming.md): **you'd be shipping
buckets, which is shipping air.** Terrible value density, full landed-cost stack
against a $12 product.

## Two claims to fix before you sell anything

### ⚠ "Chemical-free" is false, and it's a legal exposure

Hydroponics feeds plants **mineral salts** — calcium nitrate, potassium
phosphate, magnesium sulphate. That *is* the chemistry. The ions are identical
to what a plant takes up from soil, but you are adding them deliberately from a
bag.

**Never print "chemical-free," "no chemicals," or "all natural"** on a
hydroponic product. It's inaccurate, and false advertising claims on food draw
attention from the FTC and state agriculture departments.

**Claims you can make honestly:**

| Claim | Basis |
|---|---|
| **Pesticide-free** | True if you don't apply any — protected growing makes this achievable |
| **No herbicides** | Nothing to weed |
| **No soil-borne pathogens** | No manure input, so no *E. coli* vector |
| **Harvested within 24 hours** | Real, and the strongest one locally |
| **~95% less water** | 3–4 L per 200 g lettuce head, measured in Hilo |
| **Grown without electricity** | True of Kratky specifically, and genuinely rare |

### ✅ "Organic" is actually available — most people get this backwards

**USDA allows organic certification of hydroponic operations**, and states it
"has been since the National Organic Program began." The National Organic
Standards Board voted **8–7 to reject** a proposal that would have disallowed
it, and a **federal court upheld** USDA's position, finding the Organic Foods
Production Act does not exclude hydroponics.

One important caveat: **the NOSB did vote against aeroponics** keeping the
organic label. **Kratky is not aeroponic** — the roots sit in a nutrient
solution with an air gap above, which is non-circulating deep water culture. It
falls on the right side of that line.

So certification is a real option if you want it. It's a cost and paperwork
decision, not a legal impossibility.

## "Self-sustainability" also needs trimming

Kratky needs **purchased nutrient salts, forever.** As established in the
[system comparison](hydroponics-vs-aquaponics.md), no soil-less method is a
closed loop — the loop is open at the nutrient inlet.

**So don't market it as self-sufficient.** The defensible claim is
**power-independent**: no pump, no electricity, no moving parts. That's real,
unusual, and much more interesting than a self-sufficiency claim you can't
support.

Ironically, the permanent nutrient dependency is *good news commercially* — it's
the recurring revenue.

## What to actually sell, ranked

### 1. Nutrients — the razorblade

Concentrated dry nutrient mix is the opposite of a bucket: **high value per
kilogram, recurring, and mandatory.** A pound of dry mix makes on the order of a
hundred gallons of solution.

| Line | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Bulk dry mix | ~$3–6/lb |
| Retail small pack | $15–25 |
| Gross margin | **60–75%** |

**⚠ Regulatory cost line:** selling fertilizer requires **state product
registration** in most US states — registering each product, publishing a
guaranteed analysis, fees, and sometimes tonnage reporting. Check with the
Hawaii Department of Agriculture before printing labels. This is a real barrier,
not a formality — but it's also a moat once you've cleared it.

Competition is real (General Hydroponics, Masterblend), so compete on
**pre-measured convenience for Kratky specifically** — sachets dosed for one
bucket — rather than on price per pound.

### 2. Live seedlings in net pots

| Line | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Cost (seed + net pot + medium + labour) | $0.30–0.60 |
| Retail | **$2–4** |
| Margin | High, repeat purchase |

Local only — they're perishable and can't ship far. Labour-intensive. But it's
the natural companion purchase to a kit, and customers come back every cycle.

### 3. Kits at near cost — as customer acquisition

**Build them locally. Do not import.**

Buy food-grade buckets or totes domestically in bulk, drill lids, add net pots.
That **skips the entire landed-cost stack** — no ocean freight, no 30% duty, no
customs entry — which is precisely why importing them fails.

| Line | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Materials, bought in bulk locally | $10–15 |
| Sell assembled, with starter nutrients + seedlings | **$35–50** |
| Margin on hardware | Thin — and that's the point |

The kit is not the business. It's how you acquire a customer for items 1 and 2.

### 4. The produce itself — but read the next section first

## The Hawaii market finding that changes the plan

The obvious assumption is: Hawaii imports nearly all its lettuce, so local
growers charge a premium. **The first half is true. The second half isn't
supported.**

| Fact | Value |
|---|---|
| Lettuce imports as share of Hawaii consumption (2019) | **91.2%** |
| Local production | 3.5 million lbs, ~**$7.2 million** |
| **Local price premium in the Honolulu market** | **None found** |

A study of **actual Honolulu market transactions** concluded there is **no local
premium for fresh lettuce.** Local growers do deliver to supermarkets and
restaurants within 24 hours of harvest, and leafy greens genuinely wilt crossing
the ocean — so the **quality** advantage is real. It just doesn't show up as a
higher price at market.

**Do not build a business plan on charging more for local commodity lettuce.**
At wholesale you'd be competing on cost against imported lettuce, which is a
hard fight for a small grower.

**Where premium does exist:** products that aren't commodity lettuce.

- **Direct-to-restaurant** relationships with specialty varieties
- **Farmers market direct-to-consumer**, where the story and freshness are
  visible
- **Living lettuce** sold root-on in the net pot — a different product, and the
  Kratky method produces it naturally
- **Microgreens** — see below. Note these are **not** Kratky: microgreens are
  grown in shallow trays and cut at 7–14 days, before true leaves. Kratky grows
  greens to maturity over 30–55 days. Both are low-tech and power-light, but
  they are different techniques

## The best produce play: microgreens

This sidesteps the commodity comparison entirely, because it isn't the same
product.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Cycle time | **7–14 days** |
| Turns per month | 2–4 |
| Wholesale price | **$20–40/lb** |
| Seed cost per tray | ~$1–3 |
| Footprint | Very small |
| Pesticides needed | None — short cycle, protected |

**Revenue per square foot is far higher than any other crop in these records**,
capital requirements are low, and restaurants buy it as a specialty garnish
rather than comparing it to imported lettuce. Labour is the main cost.

**One regulatory distinction worth knowing: microgreens and sprouts are not
treated the same.** Under the FSMA Produce Safety Rule, **sprouts** carry a
dedicated set of much stricter requirements — seed treatment and testing —
because of past outbreak history. **Microgreens** are cut above the growing
medium and are handled as ordinary produce. So if you're selling, **grow
microgreens, not sprouts.** Confirm the current requirements and your scale
exemption with the Hawaii Department of Agriculture.

(Sprouts remain the better choice for your *own* [preparedness
use](seeds-and-propagation.md) — the regulatory burden only applies to selling.)

## Recommendation

**The business is microgreens direct to restaurants, plus nutrients and
seedlings as a local retail line. Kratky kits are a marketing device, built
locally and sold near cost.**

Ranked by how much money is actually in it:

| Priority | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Microgreens, direct to restaurants** | Highest revenue per sq ft, 7–14 day cycles, escapes the commodity price comparison, low capital |
| **2** | **Nutrients** (pre-dosed for Kratky) | Recurring, 60–75% margin, high value per kg — clear the state fertilizer registration first |
| **3** | **Seedlings + living lettuce** | High margin, repeat, and Kratky produces root-on lettuce naturally |
| **4** | Kratky kits, locally built | Near cost, as customer acquisition |
| **Never** | **Importing Kratky hardware** | Shipping buckets across an ocean against a $12 DIY alternative |

**And fix the messaging before anything else:** lead with **"pesticide-free,
harvested today, grown without electricity."** Drop "chemical-free" entirely,
drop "self-sufficient," and treat organic certification as an available option
rather than an obstacle.
